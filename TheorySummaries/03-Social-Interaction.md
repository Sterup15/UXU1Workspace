# Social Interaction

*Not yet covered in a dedicated lecture as of 2026-09-07 — this summary is built from the course textbook (Preece, Sharp & Rogers, Ch.5 "Social Interaction"). Update once the professor's own slides exist.*

## Why it's a UX topic

Course competency goal: *"Propose innovative solutions for enhancing social interactions in digital products."* People are inherently social — we live, work, learn, and play together — and a large share of modern technology exists specifically to let people stay social while physically apart (smartphones, video chat, social media, gaming, messaging, telepresence).

## Being social

Face-to-face conversation remains central, but people now spend hours a day socializing through mediated channels (texting, social media, Slack/Teams, WhatsApp). Key design questions this raises:

- Do face-to-face conversational norms (turn-taking, greetings, etiquette) transfer to mediated channels, or do new norms emerge?
- How do people choose *which* channel/app to use for a given social purpose, and how do groups switch between channels mid-conversation (texting → calling → group chat)?
- What's lost vs. gained: face-to-face conversation is spontaneous, rich in nonverbal cues (eye contact, gesture, tone), and unpredictable; text-based conversation is more premeditated/editable but loses intimacy and immediacy. Sherry Turkle's critique (2015) is that convenience is displacing the "art of conversation" and reducing opportunities to practice empathy.
- On the positive side, some technologies are explicitly *designed* to encourage social interaction — e.g., smart-speaker "skills" that support multiple people playing/choosing together, and the physical placement of a shared device (kitchen counter) affording joint/family use versus a personal phone affording solo use.

## Conversation mechanisms

Face-to-face conversation relies on tacit mechanisms: turn-taking, adjacency pairs (question→answer), back-channel signals (nodding, "mm-hm"), repair (clarifying misunderstandings), and non-verbal cues. Remote/computer-mediated conversation has had to re-invent equivalents (typing indicators, read receipts, emoji, reactions) — often imperfectly.

## Presence: telepresence, social presence, co-presence

- **Telepresence** — the feeling of being virtually present in another (real) physical location, e.g. a telepresence robot/videoconference room with someone in a remote meeting room.
- **Social presence** — the degree of awareness, feeling, and reaction to *other people* who are virtually present in an online/virtual environment (e.g., feeling like you're really "there" with someone via avatars in VR). Distinct from telepresence: telepresence is about one party projecting into a physical space; social presence is about the felt connection with other (possibly also virtual) people.
- **Co-presence** — supporting people who *are* in the same physical space to collaborate more effectively using shared technology (e.g., multitouch Smartboards/Surfaces, Kinect-based gesture systems) — requires understanding the coordination/awareness mechanisms people already use face-to-face and replicating or augmenting them.

## Social engagement

Participation in a social group's activities, often voluntary/unpaid social exchange, increasingly mediated online:
- Pro-social platforms (e.g., connecting volunteers with people who need help).
- Viral/large-scale engagement (retweets, hashtag-driven disaster response, citizen science and citizen engagement platforms) — technology allows social engagement to happen "at scale," coordinating thousands to millions of people around a shared interest or crisis.
- Trade-off: scale and immediacy (e.g., real-time disaster updates on Twitter faster than official channels) vs. reliability (rumors, outdated info spreading just as fast as accurate info).

## Design implications

- Support the *specific* conversational/coordination mechanisms relevant to your product's context — don't assume text chat can just substitute for voice or face-to-face.
- Consider where a device is placed and who it's "for" — shared physical placement invites joint/social use; personal devices invite solo use.
- When designing collaborative/social features, think about awareness (who else is here, what are they doing) and coordination (turn-taking, shared control) mechanisms explicitly.
- Weigh the ethics of engineering virality or "engagement at scale" against risks like misinformation and reduced well-being (excessive phone use, "digital bubbles").

## Key exam talking points

- Clearly distinguish telepresence vs. social presence vs. co-presence with an example of each.
- Discuss a concrete case (family sitting together each on their own phone = "digital bubbles") to illustrate tension between connecting *and* isolating effects of social technology.
- Connect to [Authenticity](04-Authenticity.md): genuine social connection vs. performative/engineered engagement (e.g., virality metrics vs. real relationship value).
