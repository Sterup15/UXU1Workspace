## What usability testing is

Usability testing measures how well real users can use a product to achieve real tasks. It's one part of the broader discipline of **evaluation**, which also includes inspection methods (heuristic evaluation, walkthroughs) and analytics (A/B testing) — but usability testing specifically centers on **watching people use the thing**.

**Standard measures** (performance-based): how long tasks take, how many errors are made, whether tasks are completed at all — plus subjective measures via satisfaction questionnaires and interviews.

## Controlled (lab) vs. natural (field) settings

This maps directly onto the course competency goal *"Compare different settings in usability testing."*

- **Controlled / lab studies** — conducted in a dedicated usability lab or a controlled setting, letting researchers hold conditions constant and directly compare variables (an **experiment**: manipulate an independent variable, measure a dependent variable). Advantage: rigor and comparability. Disadvantage: artificial — behavior in a lab may not match real-world behavior.
- **Field studies / natural settings** — conducted where people actually use the product (home, workplace, shopping mall, "in the wild"). Advantage: ecologically valid, reveals real behavior and unexpected use cases. Disadvantage: harder to control, messier data, harder to isolate cause and effect.
- Many modern setups sit in between: **mobile/remote usability testing kits** ("lab-in-a-box" / "lab-in-a-suitcase") pack cameras, laptops, and eye-trackers into a portable kit that can be set up on-site — less artificial than a fixed lab, more controlled than a pure field study. **Living labs** are another middle ground: real environments instrumented for ongoing study.

## Instrumented / physiological testing (eye-tracking & neuro-sensors)

Beyond watching and asking, usability tests can capture **direct physiological/behavioral signals**:

- **Eye-tracking** (e.g., Tobii Glasses) — records where attention actually goes, not just where people *say* they looked. Example study: researchers used mobile eye-tracking glasses to test whether shoppers in a mall actually noticed large digital displays — participants weren't told the displays were the real subject of study, keeping the task ecologically authentic.
- **Physiological sensors** — skin conductance/sweat (arousal), heart rate, breathing rate — used to infer engagement, excitement, or stress that participants might not self-report accurately. Example study: comparing physiological arousal when playing a game against a friend vs. against the computer — physiological data plus a satisfaction questionnaire together revealed higher excitement playing against a friend, going beyond what self-report alone could show.
- These "neuro/biometric" methods matter because **what people say can differ from what they do/feel** — self-report is subject to memory, social desirability, and limited introspective access; instrumented measures capture responses users may not be consciously aware of. This directly supports the course's knowledge goal: *"Recognize the impact of cognitive load on usability"* — cognitive load itself is often invisible to the user but measurable via these signals.

## The evaluation cycle

Evaluation is not a one-off final check — it's woven through the whole design process (concept sketches → low-fidelity prototypes → high-fidelity prototypes → live product), each stage suited to different evaluation methods (a paper prototype can't be "eye-tracked" meaningfully, but it can be walked through with a user).

## Design implications / good practice

- Match the setting to the question: use controlled studies when you need to compare precise alternatives; use field studies when you need to understand real-world context and unexpected use.
- Combine subjective (satisfaction, interviews) and objective (task success, time, errors, physiological) measures — they often tell different, complementary stories.
- Keep the true purpose of a study hidden when necessary to preserve ecological validity (as in the mall eye-tracking study), while still meeting informed-consent obligations.
- Usability labs remain valuable even when remote/cheap alternatives exist, because they let a whole team observe and discuss live — a social, shared-understanding benefit beyond the raw data.
