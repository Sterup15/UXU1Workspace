# Emotional Design

*Not yet covered in a dedicated lecture as of 2026-09-07 — this summary is built from the course textbook (Preece, Sharp & Rogers, Ch.6 "Emotional Interaction") so you have a grounded starting point before that lecture happens. Update with the professor's own examples once the slides exist.*

## Why emotion matters in UX

The course description's knowledge goal is explicit: *"Explain how sensed elements (colors, typography, imagery) evoke emotions"* and *"Explain emotional design using a 3 layer model."* This is a direct pointer to Don Norman's model below.

## The three-level model (Norman / Ortony, Norman & Revelle, 2005)

Emotional response is modeled as three "levels" of brain processing:

1. **Visceral level** — the lowest, prewired, automatic level. Responds instantly to sensory stimuli: is this good/bad, safe/dangerous, pleasurable/abhorrent? Triggers immediate emotional/physiological reactions (fear, joy, disgust). In design terms: **how a product looks, feels, and sounds.**
2. **Behavioral level** — where most everyday activity happens: well-learned, routine use (typing, swimming, navigating a familiar app). In design terms: **usability** — does it work, is it functional and effective?
3. **Reflective level** — conscious thought: stepping back, generalizing, interpreting meaning. In design terms: **the personal/cultural meaning and value of a product** — what it says about the owner, memories/associations it carries, brand story.

**Classic example (from the textbook):** a Swatch watch. Visceral level = its bright colors and playful design catch your eye. Behavioral level = its usability as a watch. Reflective level = the cultural meaning/self-image of wearing a Swatch. All three combine into the brand's distinct appeal.

### How sensed elements evoke emotion
- **Color** — associations with mood, brand, warnings/safety (visceral).
- **Typography** — perceived formality, friendliness, trustworthiness, legibility affecting frustration/comfort.
- **Imagery/graphics** — cultural symbols and imagery operate at the reflective level (meaning), while raw visual polish/aesthetics operates at the visceral level.

## Design across the three levels — practical implications

- **Visceral design**: aesthetics, sound design, tactile feel, first-impression "wow."
- **Behavioral design**: this is exactly what [Usability Testing](07-Usability-Testing.md) validates — does the product actually work well in use?
- **Reflective design**: storytelling, brand identity, personalization — connects directly to [Authenticity](04-Authenticity.md).

## When emotional design goes wrong: annoying interfaces

The textbook devotes a section to interfaces that *inadvertently* trigger negative emotion — frustration, anger, feeling stupid or insulted. Common triggers:
- App doesn't work / crashes
- System doesn't do what the user wants
- Unmet expectations
- Vague or obtuse error messages
- Visual noise, gimmicks, or condescension ("cute" over-friendly agents)

**Case studies of failed attempts to "warm up" an interface**: Microsoft's *Bob* (cartoon home-metaphor for new computer users) and *Clippy* (the animated paperclip assistant) were both meant to reduce anxiety at the visceral/behavioral level but were widely experienced as patronizing or intrusive — a reminder that emotional design has to be earned through genuine usefulness, not decorated on top ([Authenticity](04-Authenticity.md) is relevant here too).

## Related concepts worth connecting

- **Affective computing / emotional AI** — systems that try to detect emotion (via facial expression, voice tone, eye-tracking, typing patterns) and respond to it.
- **Persuasive technology & behavioral change** — using emotional/psychological design to nudge behavior (fitness trackers, habit apps) — raises ethical questions about manipulation.
- **Anthropomorphism** — giving software human-like qualities (voice assistants, avatars) to trigger social/emotional responses — a double-edged tool per the Bob/Clippy examples above.
- **Flow** (Csikszentmihalyi) — a state of complete emotional absorption in an activity, often used as a design goal for engaging experiences (games, immersive websites).

## Key exam talking points

- Be able to draw/describe the three-level model and map a real product (or your own course-assignment design) onto all three levels.
- Explain *why* Clippy/Bob failed in terms of the model (arguably strong visceral/behavioral intent but broken reflective-level trust — felt patronizing rather than genuinely useful).
- Connect emotional design explicitly to [Hedonics](05-Hedonics.md) (pleasure/stimulation) and [Authenticity](04-Authenticity.md) (genuine vs. manufactured emotional appeal).
