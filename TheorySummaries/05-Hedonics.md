## What "hedonic" means in UX

**Hedonics** = the study of pleasure — in UX, the *non-instrumental*, experiential quality of using a product: is it enjoyable, stimulating, engaging — as opposed to merely functional.

## Hassenzahl's model: pragmatic vs. hedonic quality

Marc Hassenzahl (2010) frames user experience as having two complementary dimensions:

- **Pragmatic quality** — how simple, practical, and effective it is for the user to achieve their goals. This is essentially classic **usability** (the "do-goals": get the task done).
- **Hedonic quality** — how evocative and stimulating the interaction is. This covers the **"be-goals"**: how the product makes the user feel about *themselves* and their experience, independent of task completion. Hedonic quality is typically broken down into:
  - **Stimulation** — satisfying the need for novelty, challenge, and growth (learning something new, being surprised).
  - **Identification** — communicating identity to oneself and others (a product as a form of self-expression, similar to the *reflective* level in [Emotional Design](01-Emotional-Design.md)).
  - **Evocation** — provoking memories, being a "souvenir" of who we are or want to be.

A product can be highly usable (pragmatic) but joyless, or highly enjoyable (hedonic) but impractical — good UX design usually aims to balance both, depending on the product's purpose (a tax-filing tool leans pragmatic; a game leans hedonic).

## Hedonic heuristics

Väänänen-Vainio-Mattila & Waljas (2009) translated hedonic *design guidelines* into evaluable heuristic *questions*, such as:
- "Is the service a lively place where it is enjoyable to spend time?"
- "Does the service satisfy users' curiosity by frequently offering interesting content?"

This shows hedonics isn't just a theoretical lens — it can be operationalized into concrete evaluation criteria, connecting it to [Usability Testing](07-Usability-Testing.md) (specifically heuristic evaluation).

## Related concept: Flow

Csikszentmihalyi's concept of **flow** — complete emotional absorption in an activity, losing track of time — is frequently used in interaction design as a hedonic design goal, especially for games, immersive websites, and creative tools. Elements that support flow: clear goals, immediate feedback, a balance of challenge and skill, a sense of control.

## Design implications

- Ask what your product's *be-goals* are, not just its do-goals: what should using it make the person feel about themselves?
- Hedonic and pragmatic quality can be designed and evaluated somewhat independently — a product might need targeted work on enjoyment even after usability problems are fixed.
- Aesthetics, playfulness, surprise, personalization, and narrative (see [Authenticity](04-Authenticity.md)) are levers for hedonic quality; task efficiency, error prevention, and learnability are levers for pragmatic quality.

## Key exam talking points

- State the pragmatic/hedonic distinction clearly and give one product example of each extreme (e.g., a boarding-pass scanner = pragmatic; a mood-tracking journal app = hedonic).
- Connect hedonics directly to [Emotional Design](01-Emotional-Design.md)'s reflective level (identification/evocation overlap heavily with "personal and cultural meaning").
