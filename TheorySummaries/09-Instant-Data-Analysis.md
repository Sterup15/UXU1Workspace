# Instant Data Analysis (IDA)

*Not yet covered in a dedicated lecture as of 2026-09-07. Note: this technique does **not** appear in the course's main textbook (Preece, Sharp & Rogers) — it comes from a separate, well-established HCI research thread (Kjeldskov, Skov & Stage, 2004), and it's worth noting that Jesper Kjeldskov (one of the method's originators) is a Danish HCI professor (Aalborg University) — plausibly why this specific technique made it onto a Danish course's curriculum even though it's absent from the assigned textbook. Update once the professor's own slides exist.*

## What it is

**Instant Data Analysis** is a lightweight, rapid method for analyzing usability test sessions **without** the time-consuming step of fully transcribing and coding video recordings. Instead of spending days per participant on detailed video analysis, evaluators extract the key usability problems **immediately after each test session, while it's still fresh in memory**.

Course competency goal it maps to: *"Analyse user feedback to improve a design using Instant Data Analysis."*

## Why it exists

Traditional usability evaluation (full video transcription, detailed coding of every interaction) is thorough but **slow and expensive** — often impractical in short, iterative development cycles (e.g., agile sprints, or a course project with limited time). IDA was developed to get **most of the value, in a fraction of the time**, so usability findings can actually feed back into the next design iteration before it's too late to matter.

## How it typically works

1. **During testing**: two observers (rather than just one) watch each usability test session live (or via video), independently taking notes on usability problems as they occur — no full transcript is made.
2. **Immediately after the session** (same day, ideally within the hour): the observers/evaluators meet and **pool their notes verbally**, going through the session from memory and notes to build a shared list of identified usability problems.
3. **Problems are consolidated and prioritized**: duplicate observations are merged, and each problem is typically rated by severity/frequency, producing a **prioritized list of usability issues** ready to hand to designers/developers.
4. This is usually repeated across a small number of participants, then the combined lists across sessions are merged into one final report.

## Strengths

- **Fast** — a usability problem list can be ready hours after testing finishes, rather than days or weeks later.
- **Cheap** — no need for expensive video-coding software or many analyst-hours per session.
- **Well-suited to iterative/agile design** — findings can inform the very next prototype iteration.
- Research (Kjeldskov, Skov & Stage) found IDA identifies a comparable proportion of *major* usability problems to full video analysis, despite the drastically reduced effort.

## Limitations

- Less rigorous/traceable than full video analysis — no complete record exists to go back and re-verify a claim later.
- Relies on evaluators' memory and note-taking skill; risks missing subtler problems that a slow, detailed pass might catch.
- Best suited to identifying and prioritizing problems quickly, not for deep academic-style qualitative analysis or precise quantitative metrics.
- Works best with **experienced evaluators** who know what to look and listen for in the moment.

## Design implications / when to use it

- Use IDA when the goal is **fast, actionable feedback** to steer the next design iteration — e.g., mid-project usability checks, not a final summative evaluation for a report or publication.
- Pair it with a small number of test participants (classic usability testing guidance: even 5 users can reveal most major usability problems) — IDA's speed advantage compounds well with small-sample iterative testing.
- Still worth supplementing with select video clips or quotes for the *most* important/surprising findings, both for a stronger case and for [Authenticity](04-Authenticity.md) when presenting results to stakeholders.

## Key exam talking points

- Contrast IDA against full video-based usability analysis: what's traded away (rigor, traceability) for what's gained (speed, cost, iteration-friendliness).
- Explain the two-observer + immediate-debrief structure and why immediacy matters (memory freshness, avoiding the cost of transcription).
- Be ready to describe how you would apply IDA to your own course assignment's usability test, including how you'd prioritize the resulting problem list.
