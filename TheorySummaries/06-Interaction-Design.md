# Interaction Design

*Source: UXU Introduction lecture (24–30 Aug 2026); Preece, Sharp & Rogers Ch.1–3.*

## What it is

**Interaction** = "an occasion when two or more people or things communicate with or react to each other." **Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)** narrows this to the human–interaction–computer triangle. **Interaction Design (ID)** is broader still — Preece/Sharp/Rogers define it as:

> Designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives.

The course slides make the point that "UX," "usability," "HCI," "user-centered design," "human factors," and "customer experience" are largely **old wine in new bottles** (Jakob Nielsen, *The Immutable Rules of UX*, 2018) — overlapping labels for the same underlying concern: designing *for* the person, not just the technology.

## UX vs. Usability vs. Interaction Design

- **Interaction Design** — the process/discipline of shaping how people and products interact.
- **Usability** — a *quality attribute* of that interaction: is it effective, efficient, and satisfying to use (see [Usability Testing](07-Usability-Testing.md))?
- **User eXperience (UX)** — the *holistic, subjective* result: "the apprehension of an object, thought, or emotion through the senses or mind" — everything the user perceives, feels, and thinks before, during, and after use, not just whether they could complete the task.

## The building blocks of this course

The intro deck frames the whole semester around these interlocking "gears": **User, Usability, Emotions, Cognition, Authenticity, Prototyping, Testing** — i.e., understanding the user's mind (cognition) and feelings (emotion, authenticity, hedonics), then validating design decisions through prototyping and testing.

## The core design cycle

A recurring pattern across the exercises so far (e.g., the "human augmentation for a student in class" exercise, the "popcorn app" exercise):

1. **Understand the user & situation** — who is it for, what's the context, constraints (time, environment, number of users)?
2. **Ideate** — imagine the new situation; don't just patch the old one ("re-think it completely — what is it you *really* want?").
3. **Prototype** — extremely simple, fast, disposable (paper, tablet mock-ups, cardboard).
4. **Test** — let someone else *use* it rather than being shown it ("don't show and tell — let them try it").
5. Iterate.

This mirrors the textbook's emphasis that design and evaluation are **interwoven, iterative processes**, not a single linear handoff.

## Key exam talking points

- Be able to state the difference between usability, UX, and interaction design in your own words.
- Use the "gears" framing (cognition, emotion, authenticity, usability, testing, prototyping) to show how the course's topics fit into one coherent design philosophy.
- Have a concrete example ready (e.g., a bad interaction design you redesigned in the first-week exercise) to anchor abstract definitions.
